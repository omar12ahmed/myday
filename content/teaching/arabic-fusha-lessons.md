## Orientation, placement and learning habits

<a id="lesson-arabic-fusha-register"></a>
### What Fuṣḥā means and where it is used

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-fusha-register`

#### Read

Fuṣḥā is the formal Arabic used in much writing, education and prepared public speech. Modern Standard Arabic is the main target here; classical and Qur'anic study are later optional branches. Everyday conversation often uses regional varieties. A speaker can mix registers, so label what you hear rather than assuming every Arabic recording teaches the same form. Reading familiar script does not by itself establish comprehension.

#### Worked example

أَيْنَ تَسْكُنُ؟ means 'Where do you live?' when addressing a man in standard Arabic. Learn the sentence as a meaningful question, including its sound, rather than only recognising its letters.

#### Try it

Write two situations where you want to use fuṣḥā. Listen to the linked introductions and identify whether you can recover names or countries.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

News reading and formal introductions are suitable examples. Record what you actually understood; leave uncertain details blank. This is a baseline, not a proficiency certificate.

</details>

### Measure your starting point without guessing

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-goal-baseline`

#### Read

Measure reading, listening, speaking and writing separately. A memorised introduction can be fluent while an unfamiliar message remains difficult. Use a short unseen sample, record the support used and repeat with a comparable new sample later. CEFR labels describe broad ability and are not awarded automatically by completing these modules.

#### Worked example

You understand a written introduction after looking up three words but cannot follow its recording. Record supported reading and limited listening separately, rather than assigning yourself one confident level.

#### Try it

Read this new sentence: أَنَا طَالِبٌ وَأَسْكُنُ فِي لَنْدَنَ. Then cover it and say what it means.

<details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-learning-loop"></a>
### Practise, retrieve and revisit

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-learning-loop`

#### Read

A useful learning loop combines understanding, noticing form, retrieval and meaningful reuse. Study a small example, hide it, reconstruct it and compare. Revisit after a delay with changed details so familiarity is not mistaken for independence. Keep vocabulary in phrases with meaning and sound.

#### Worked example

Learn هٰذَا كِتَابٌ, 'This is a book.' Hide it, say it, then replace كِتَابٌ with قَلَمٌ, 'pen'. The change tests whether you understand the sentence pattern.

#### Try it

Make a three-card practice set for 'this is a book', 'this is a pen' and 'is this a book?'

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Use هٰذَا كِتَابٌ، هٰذَا قَلَمٌ، هَلْ هٰذَا كِتَابٌ؟ Compare meaning and form, then attempt a new object tomorrow.

</details>

### Set up readable Arabic and a recording notebook

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-arabic-tools`

#### Read

Arabic runs right to left, while numbers and embedded Latin text may behave differently. Use a readable Arabic font and keyboard, and preserve the original spelling when copying. Keep recordings labelled with date, task and whether you used a script. A recording is practice evidence; automatic transcription is not a reliable pronunciation examiner.

#### Worked example

A note contains بَيْتٌ = 'house' and the date in a separate field. Separating languages and metadata makes it easier to read than forcing everything into one mixed-direction sentence.

#### Try it

Type بَيْت، كِتَاب، طَالِب. Read each back and record one short attempt using your device's recorder if available.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

The words mean house, book and student. Check letter order and joining; compare a human model for sound. Keep a text-only task if recording is unavailable.

</details>

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-practice-contract`

A sustainable session has one communicative goal and a clear stopping point. On low-energy days, listen to one short model and retrieve one phrase. #### Worked example

A ten-minute session: understand two introductions, retrieve one without looking, then record a single correction. A longer session adds a new person and a short exchange.

## Arabic script and decoding

<a id="lesson-arabic-letter-families"></a>
### Recognise all 28 basic letter shapes

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-letter-families`

#### Read

Arabic's basic alphabet has 28 letters. Several share a skeleton and differ by dots, such as ب ت ث. Learn shape, sound and position together. Letter names help identify symbols, but reading requires their sounds inside words. Similar-looking letters are not interchangeable.

#### Worked example

ب has one dot below, ت two above, and ث three above. In بَابٌ, the two ب letters surround a long ā written with ا. The word means 'door'.

#### Try it

Sort ب، ث، ت، ن، ي by dot placement, then identify the two repeated letters in بَابٌ.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

ب repeats. ب has one dot below; ت two above; ث three above; ن one above; ي normally two below in these forms. Compare their connected forms in the chart.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-letter-joining"></a>
### Read connected forms and joining breaks

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-letter-joining`

#### Read

Most Arabic letters connect to neighbouring letters, changing appearance by position. ا د ذ ر ز و do not connect to the following letter on their left. A joining break inside a word is not a space or a new word. Learn connected words, not only isolated alphabet cards.

#### Worked example

كِتَابٌ is one word even though ا does not connect to the final ب. بَيْتٌ has connected forms for bāʾ, yāʾ and tāʾ. Both must be recognised as whole written words.

#### Try it

Copy بَيْت، كِتَاب، وَرْد and mark internal joining breaks without adding spaces.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

كِتَاب breaks after ا; وَرْد has breaks after و and ر. Preserve the word as one unit. Compare each connected shape with its isolated letter.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-vowel-decoding"></a>
### Short vowels, long vowels and syllables

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-vowel-decoding`

#### Read

Short vowels are fatḥa ـَ (a), kasra ـِ (i) and ḍamma ـُ (u). Long ā, ī and ū are commonly written with ا، ي، و in the relevant vowel patterns. Length changes words, so do not treat it as optional emphasis. Read consonant-plus-vowel units before attempting a whole unfamiliar word.

#### Worked example

كَتَبَ, kataba, means 'he wrote'; كَاتِبٌ, kātibun, means 'a writer'. The long ā and different pattern matter. Transliteration here supports noticing; the Arabic script remains the target.

#### Try it

Read بَ، بِ، بُ, then بَا، بِي، بُو. Explain what changed.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

The consonant remains b; vowel quality changes across a/i/u, and the second set lengthens each vowel. Compare a human recording rather than guessing duration from English spelling.

</details>

#### Read, watch or explore next

- **Watch / listen:** [Arabic Reading Lesson 1: Arabic Alphabet [with Interactive Chart] - Madinah Arabic – Madinah Arabic](https://madinaharabic.com/free-content/reading/lesson-1/part-1) — Use the short- and long-vowel sections of the reading course and repeat its sound models.
  Public page at review; optional paid services are not assigned.

<a id="lesson-arabic-sukun-shadda"></a>
### Sukūn, shadda and the visible word ending

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-sukun-shadda`

#### Read

Sukūn ـْ indicates no following short vowel on that consonant. Shadda ـّ marks a doubled consonant, held across a syllable boundary. Tanwīn ـٌ ـٍ ـً often marks an indefinite noun's ending in fully inflected connected reading. Pausal pronunciation can differ, so learn the written form and the model's context.

#### Worked example

دَرَسَ means 'he studied'; دَرَّسَ means 'he taught'. The doubled r is meaningful. In بَيْتٌ, the sukūn on ي helps show the diphthong ay rather than a long ī.

#### Try it

Identify the doubled sound in مُعَلِّمٌ, 'teacher', and the consonant without a following vowel in مَكْتَبٌ, 'office/desk'.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

ل is doubled in مُعَلِّمٌ; ك has sukūn in مَكْتَبٌ. Do not insert an extra vowel after every consonant.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-special-spellings"></a>
### Hamza, tāʾ marbūṭa and alif maqṣūra

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-special-spellings`

#### Read

Hamza ء represents a consonant and may sit on ا، و or ي or stand alone. Tāʾ marbūṭa ة commonly ends feminine nouns and has different connected and pausal realisations. Alif maqṣūra ى normally represents a final long ā, not the y sound of ي. These distinctions affect reading and spelling.

#### Worked example

مَدْرَسَةٌ means 'school'; its final ة is not ه. مُوسَى ends in a long ā represented by ى. أَكَلَ, 'he ate', begins with pronounced hamza.

#### Try it

Match ة، ى، ء to tāʾ marbūṭa, alif maqṣūra and hamza; give one example from the lesson.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

ة → مَدْرَسَةٌ; ى → مُوسَى; ء appears on its seat in أَكَلَ. Learn hamza spelling gradually with actual words.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-script-meaning"></a>
### From accurate decoding to actual meaning

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-script-meaning`

#### Read

Decoding turns letters into a plausible sound; comprehension connects the word to meaning and use. Check both. A learner can accurately read a word aloud without knowing what it refers to. Use small meaningful contrasts and pictures or objects, then retrieve the word without the model.

#### Worked example

هٰذَا بَابٌ means 'This is a door'; هٰذَا كِتَابٌ means 'This is a book'. Correctly sounding out بَابٌ does not answer which object it names.

#### Try it

Read هٰذَا قَلَمٌ. Point to the appropriate object or draw it, then cover the sentence and reconstruct it.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

'This is a pen.' Successful practice combines decoding, meaning and retrieval. If one fails, practise that component specifically.

</details>

## Listening foundations and intelligible pronunciation

<a id="lesson-arabic-listening-noticing"></a>
### Hear before imitating

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-listening-noticing`

#### Read

Listening begins with noticing boundaries and contrasts, not repeating everything immediately. Hear a short segment, predict meaning, listen again for one feature, then check the transcript. Reading first can make you believe you heard words that the text supplied. Keep unsupported listening and supported listening separate.

#### Worked example

On a first pass you recognise أَنَا, 'I', but miss the profession. On a second pass you identify طَبِيبَةٌ, 'a female doctor'. Checking afterward shows which information you actually recovered.

#### Try it

Use one speaker's introduction in the linked dialogue. Write only words you heard before showing the transcript.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Compare your record with the text and replay one missed phrase. A blank is more useful than guessing and later treating the guess as evidence.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-length-contrast"></a>
### Hear and produce vowel length and gemination

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-length-contrast`

#### Read

Vowel length and consonant doubling can distinguish meanings. Long vowels should remain longer than short ones within the speaker's rhythm; shadda doubles the consonant, not the following vowel. Practise perception and production separately with a reliable model. Text-only self-checking cannot establish pronunciation accuracy.

#### Worked example

عَلِمَ means 'he knew'; عَلَّمَ means 'he taught'. The second has doubled l. كَتَبَ and كَاتِبٌ also differ in pattern and vowel length.

#### Try it

Mark the long vowel in كَاتِبٌ and the doubled consonant in مُدَرِّسٌ. Record both after a model if possible.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

ا marks the long ā in كَاتِبٌ; ر is doubled in مُدَرِّسٌ. Compare duration and syllable shape, not just loudness.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-throat-sounds"></a>
### Distinguish hamza, hāʾ, ḥāʾ, ʿayn, khāʾ and ghayn

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-throat-sounds`

#### Read

ء، ه، ح، ع، خ، غ represent distinct consonants in standard Arabic. English spelling approximations are limited: ح is not simply a louder h, and ع is not a vowel to omit. Use short human demonstrations and comfortable imitation, focusing on one contrast at a time. Do not force or strain the throat.

#### Worked example

هُوَ, 'he', begins with ه; حُبٌّ, 'love', begins with ح. غ differs from خ in voicing. Recognising the written letter and hearing the sound are separate tasks.

#### Try it

Compare the linked ح and خ demonstrations, then label which letter each clip teaches without looking at its title.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

ح is a pharyngeal fricative; خ is a velar/uvular fricative. If you cannot distinguish them yet, repeat listening and seek targeted feedback rather than claiming mastery from spelling.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-emphatics-qaf"></a>
### Emphatic sounds, qāf and interdental consonants

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-emphatics-qaf`

#### Read

Standard Arabic distinguishes س/ص، ت/ط، د/ض، ذ/ظ and ق/ك. Emphatic consonants affect the surrounding sound quality; qāf is articulated farther back than kāf. ث، ذ، ظ are also distinct from ت، د، ز in the standard target. Regional pronunciations vary, so compare with the fuṣḥā model.

#### Worked example

كَلْبٌ means 'dog'; قَلْبٌ means 'heart'. The first consonant changes the word. سَيْفٌ means 'sword', while صَيْفٌ means 'summer'.

#### Try it

Identify the contrasting first letters in those pairs and practise recognising them before producing them.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

The pairs are ك/ق and س/ص. Use the linked sound model and a tutor's feedback for production; spelling alone cannot verify the contrast.

</details>

#### Read

<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-negation-system`

#### Read

Choose negation by construction and time. لَا commonly negates a present action; لَمْ with a jussive present form negates a past action; لَنْ with a subjunctive present form negates future action. لَيْسَ negates many nominal statements. These patterns are not interchangeable prefixes.

#### Worked example

لَا أَكْتُبُ, 'I do not write'; لَمْ أَكْتُبْ, 'I did not write'; لَنْ أَكْتُبَ, 'I will not write'. Notice the final vowels in the model.

<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

</details>

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-requests-imperatives`

Imperatives give instructions; polite requests can soften or reframe them. The form changes with the addressee. Prohibition commonly uses لَا with a jussive present verb. Do not assume the affirmative imperative can be negated simply by placing لَا before it.

<a id="lesson-arabic-pronoun-attachment"></a>
### Attach pronouns to nouns, verbs and prepositions

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-pronoun-attachment`

#### Read

Attached pronouns can express possession after a noun, an object after a verb, or a complement after a preposition. Their function depends on the host word. Some spellings and vowels change in context. Recognise the stem and suffix before looking up the whole surface form.

#### Worked example

كِتَابُهُ is 'his book'; رَأَيْتُهُ is 'I saw him'; مَعَهُ is 'with him'. The same ـهُ points to a masculine singular referent but fills different grammatical roles.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

'My book', 'I saw you' addressing a man, and 'with us'. The suffix is possessive, object and prepositional complement respectively.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-case-roles"></a>
### Recognise subject, object and prepositional roles

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-case-roles`

#### Read

Case endings help signal grammatical roles in fully inflected Arabic. Subjects are often nominative, direct objects accusative and nouns after prepositions genitive. These are starting patterns, not a complete parsing system. Unvowelled writing hides many endings, so syntax and meaning remain essential.

#### Worked example

قَرَأَ الطَّالِبُ الْكِتَابَ فِي الْبَيْتِ means 'The student read the book at home'. ـُ marks the subject, ـَ the object, and ـِ the noun after فِي in this regular example.

#### Try it

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

الطَّالِبُ is the reader; الْكِتَابَ is what was read; فِي الْبَيْتِ gives location. Roles explain the endings rather than the other way around.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-idafa-chains"></a>
### Longer possession phrases and adjective attachment

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-idafa-chains`

#### Read

An iḍāfa can contain several nouns. Determine the relationships before adding adjectives, because adjective agreement and position help show what is being described. The first nouns in an iḍāfa chain do not take ال or tanwīn. Definiteness can come from the final definite element.

#### Worked example

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

بَابُ غُرْفَةِ الطَّالِبِ. Trace door → room → student, and do not insert ال on the first two nouns.

<a id="lesson-arabic-dual-sound-plurals"></a>
### Two things and regular plural endings

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-dual-sound-plurals`

#### Read

The dual marks two, commonly ـانِ in nominative and ـيْنِ in accusative/genitive. Sound masculine plurals commonly use ـونَ / ـينَ; sound feminine plurals use ـات with their own case pattern. Learn role and ending together. These suffixes do not replace broken plurals indiscriminately.

#### Worked example

<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

<a id="lesson-arabic-root-pattern"></a>
### See word families without guessing meaning

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-root-pattern`

#### Read

Many Arabic words combine a root with a pattern, but a shared root does not guarantee an identical meaning. Recognising a family helps lookup and memory; dictionaries and context establish the actual sense. Some letters belong to patterns or attached particles rather than the root.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

The shared root is ك ت ب. Translating all three as 'writing' would lose book, person and place meanings. Verify the actual word in context.

</details>

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-claim-evidence`

#### Read

A claim states something the writer wants accepted; evidence is offered to support it. Examples illustrate but may not establish a general rule. Ask whether evidence is relevant, sufficient and representative. Separate your agreement with the conclusion from the strength of its support.

### Verb-first and subject-first agreement

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-word-order-agreement`

#### Read

Standard Arabic often distinguishes verb-first and subject-first agreement with an overt plural subject. </details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-hal-tamyiz"></a>
### Circumstance and specification

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-hal-tamyiz`

#### Read

Ḥāl describes the circumstance or state of a participant during an action; tamyīz clarifies an otherwise underspecified quantity or relation. Both can appear accusative, but their functions differ. Determine what question the phrase answers before assigning a label.

#### Worked example

عَادَ الطَّالِبُ مُتْعَبًا means 'The student returned tired': مُتْعَبًا gives the student's state. اِشْتَرَيْتُ عِشْرِينَ كِتَابًا means 'I bought twenty books': كِتَابًا specifies what is counted.

#### Try it

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

'In what state did he return?' points to ḥāl; 'twenty of what?' points to tamyīz. An ending alone does not establish function.

</details>

### Fuller number patterns and restricted declension

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-numbers-exceptions`

#### Read

### Read dense disciplinary prose strategically

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-academic-reading`

#### Read

Dense academic prose often packs relationships into noun phrases, definitions and qualifications. Identify the research question or explanatory purpose, mark key terms and reconstruct the argument before looking up every word. Distinguish author claims, cited claims and your interpretation.

#### Worked example

تشير النتائج إلى احتمال وجود علاقة means 'The results indicate the possibility of a relationship'. It does not say the relationship is certain or causal. The qualification belongs in your notes.

#### Try it

Read a dense paragraph and write its claim, evidence type and one limitation in separate sentences.

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-lecture-notes`

#### Read

Formal listening requires recognising the speaker's structure while selecting essential information. Prepare the topic vocabulary, then note main points, examples and transitions with timestamps. Do not transcribe every word at the expense of understanding. Review notes soon after listening and mark gaps for targeted replay.

#### Worked example

A speaker introduces a historical period, gives two examples and draws a conclusion. Notes organised under those functions are easier to reconstruct than disconnected nouns.

<a id="lesson-arabic-synthesis-citation"></a>
### Synthesize sources and attribute claims carefully

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-synthesis-citation`

#### Read

Synthesis relates sources while preserving who said what. Separate agreement, difference and your own interpretation. A citation should identify the actual source used, not a source merely mentioned by another writer. Quotations require exact checking; paraphrases still require attribution when they convey sourced claims.

#### Worked example

A modern article quotes a traveller. If you have read only the article, say that it quotes the traveller; do not imply you inspected the original edition.

#### Try it

Write a paragraph connecting a modern report and a historical account, with explicit attribution and one limitation.

<details>

Keep the sources' dates, genres and evidential status distinct. Mark your connecting interpretation and avoid claiming one independently verifies the other.

</details>

### Write for a decision and defend the recommendation

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-professional-brief`

#### Read

A professional brief answers a decision question with a recommendation, reasons, risks and next action. Put essential information early and make responsibilities clear. Use concise fuṣḥā suited to the audience. Do not hide uncertainty behind technical vocabulary or imply evidence you do not have.

## Literary Arabic, stories and style

<a id="lesson-arabic-literary-selection"></a>
### Choose an accessible text and identify its setting

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-literary-selection`

#### Read

Choose a literary passage with manageable language, a clear edition and a purpose for reading. Start with a short complete scene or fable rather than an entire difficult book. Identify narrator, setting and central problem before analysing style. Classical prose and modern narrative may require different support.

#### Worked example

Original micro-story: فَتَحَتْ لَيْلَى النَّافِذَةَ، فَعَادَ صَوْتُ الْمَدِينَةِ إِلَى الْغُرْفَةِ. 'Layla opened the window, and the city's sound returned to the room.' The action and sensory detail establish a scene.

#### Try it

Describe the action and setting in the micro-story, then sample the opening of the assigned fable.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Do not infer a whole biography or emotional state from one sentence. Select a short passage you can revisit with a dictionary and commentary.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-narrator-character"></a>
### Follow narrator, characters and time

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-narrator-character`

#### Read

Distinguish narrator, character speech and the author's historical identity. Track pronouns, speech verbs and time shifts. A character's belief is not necessarily the narrator's judgement. Reliable interpretation begins with knowing who speaks and what each participant knows.

#### Worked example

Original example: قال سامر: سأعود غدًا. لكن الراوي يذكر أنه لم يعد. 'Samir said: I will return tomorrow. But the narrator states that he did not return.' Promise and narrated outcome differ.

#### Try it

Mark speakers and pronoun referents in a short scene, then summarise the event order.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Keep direct speech, reported speech and narration separate. If a referent is ambiguous, explain the alternatives rather than silently choosing one.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-imagery-style"></a>
### Interpret imagery and stylistic choices

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-imagery-style`

#### Read

Imagery uses sensory or figurative language to shape how a scene is understood. First establish literal meaning, then explain the comparison or association and its effect in context. Multiple interpretations may be possible, but each needs textual support. Do not substitute a memorised symbolism list for reading.

#### Worked example

Original example: ابتلعت المدينةُ صوته, 'The city swallowed his voice', gives the city an action associated with a living creature. It can suggest overwhelming noise, depending on context.

#### Try it

Explain the image, one plausible effect and one interpretation the sentence alone cannot establish.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Identify the figurative action and relate it to the scene. The sentence does not by itself prove a political or psychological intention by the author.

</details>

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-poetry-entry`

Begin a poem by identifying speaker, literal sense, images, repetitions and line relationships. Rhythm and rhyme can contribute to meaning, but recognising them requires an appropriate model. Do not force prose word order onto every poetic construction or treat every line as an isolated proverb.

#### Worked example

In an original image, 'the morning opens a window for hope', literal opening is transferred to an abstract possibility. A reading should explain that transfer and how surrounding lines develop it.

## Bridge from MSA to classical prose

<a id="lesson-arabic-classical-register"></a>
### Recognise what changes across periods and genres

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-classical-register`

#### Read

Classical texts share much with modern fuṣḥā but differ by period, genre, vocabulary and conventions. A modern familiar sense may be wrong in an older context. Identify the text's provenance and use a suitable dictionary or commentary. Do not assume classical prose, poetry and Qur'anic language are interchangeable registers.

#### Worked example

سَيَّارَة in some older contexts can refer to a travelling group, while modern everyday use commonly means a car. The right reading depends on period and passage.

#### Try it

Choose one familiar-looking word in a classical passage and verify whether its modern sense fits.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Record the historical sense, context and reference used. Keep alternative senses if the evidence does not settle the reading.

</details>

<a id="lesson-arabic-classical-parsing"></a>
### Use iʿrāb to support meaning

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-classical-parsing`

#### Read

Iʿrāb analyses grammatical function and inflection to support interpretation. Start with clause structure, verb, participants and attachments, then examine endings. Parsing is useful when it resolves a real ambiguity; naming every category without recovering meaning is incomplete.

#### Worked example

ضَرَبَ زَيْدٌ عَمْرًا, 'Zayd struck Amr', distinguishes subject and object through endings. In a different word order, endings can still preserve those roles.

#### Try it

Explain who acts in ضَرَبَ عَمْرًا زَيْدٌ and why.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the explained answer</summary>

Zayd remains the subject with nominative ـٌ; Amr remains the object with accusative ـًا. Do not identify the actor solely by first position after the verb.

</details>

A word can acquire a specialised scientific sense after having a broader everyday meaning. Reading an old passage through only the specialised modern sense can distort its claim.

Editions can differ in wording, punctuation, vocalisation and editorial choices. A commentary offers an interpretation and may report alternatives. #### Worked example

Two editions punctuate a sentence differently, changing which phrase appears attached to a clause. Before declaring one reading obvious, inspect editorial notes and the underlying wording.

<a id="lesson-arabic-translation-choices"></a>

Lesson ID: `lesson.arabic-translation-choices`

#### Read

Translation preserves relationships and intended meaning, not merely word order. Idioms, collocations and technical terms may require different structures. Compare alternatives against the brief and context. Keep a translator's explanatory addition distinct from the source's own claims.

#### Worked example
